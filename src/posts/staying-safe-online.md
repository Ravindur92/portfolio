Most people picture a cyberattack as a hooded figure breaking through firewalls in a dark room. In reality, the most common attacks are far less dramatic: a convincing email, a password reused on five sites, an app that has not been updated in a year.

Attackers are opportunists. They go for the **easiest door**, not the strongest lock. That works in your favour, because you do not need to be an expert to protect yourself. You just need to be a harder target than the next person.

> **The goal is not perfect security.** It is to make yourself expensive and annoying enough that attackers move on to someone else.

Here are ten habits that do exactly that.

---

## 1. Give every account its own strong password

When one website is breached, attackers take the leaked email and password pairs and try them everywhere else. This is called **credential stuffing**, and it works because people reuse passwords.

- Use a **unique password for every account**, especially email, banking and social media.
- Make them long. A passphrase of four or five random words is stronger and easier to remember than a short, complicated one.
- Use a **password manager** so you only have to remember one strong master password.
- Check whether your email has appeared in a known data breach at haveibeenpwned.com, and change any password that has.

Your email account is the most important one. Whoever controls it can reset the password on almost everything else.

## 2. Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA)

MFA means a stolen password alone is no longer enough. The attacker also needs a second proof that only you have.

- Turn it on for email, banking, cloud storage and social media first.
- **Best:** passkeys or a hardware security key.
- **Good:** an authenticator app that generates short-lived codes.
- **Better than nothing:** SMS codes. They can be intercepted or hijacked through SIM swapping, but they still stop most basic attacks.

Never read an MFA code out loud to someone who calls you. No real bank or company will ask for it.

## 3. Learn to spot phishing

Phishing is the number one way people get compromised. It arrives by email, SMS (smishing), phone calls (vishing) and even QR codes. The message is designed to make you act before you think.

**Common red flags:**

- **Urgency or fear:** "Your account will be closed in 24 hours."
- **Unexpected requests** for passwords, codes, payments or gift cards.
- **A sender address that is slightly off**, like `support@paypa1.com`.
- **Links that do not match** where they claim to go. Hover over a link before clicking.
- **Unexpected attachments**, especially invoices, zip files and Office documents asking you to "enable macros".

**What to do instead:** do not click anything in the message. Open the official website or app yourself, or call the number printed on your card or on the company's real website. If it was a work or school email, report it to your IT team.

## 4. Keep everything updated

Updates are not just new features. Many of them fix security holes that attackers already know about and are actively exploiting.

- Turn on **automatic updates** for your phone, computer, browser and apps.
- Restart when asked. Many fixes do not take effect until you do.
- Stop using devices and software that no longer receive security updates.

## 5. Secure your devices and your Wi-Fi

- Use a **screen lock** (PIN, fingerprint or face) on every phone and laptop.
- Turn on **device encryption**, like BitLocker on Windows or FileVault on Mac, so a lost laptop does not mean lost data.
- On your home router, **change the default admin password**, use WPA2 or WPA3 encryption, and install firmware updates.
- On public Wi-Fi, avoid logging in to sensitive accounts. Mobile data is safer, and a reputable VPN adds some protection on untrusted networks, though it is not a magic shield.

## 6. Back up your data, and keep one copy offline

Ransomware locks your files and demands payment to unlock them. The best defence is not paying, it is **not needing to**.

Follow the **3-2-1 rule**:

- **3** copies of your important data
- **2** different types of storage (for example, your computer and an external drive)
- **1** copy stored somewhere else, ideally offline or in the cloud

An external drive that stays plugged in can be encrypted by ransomware along with everything else, so disconnect it after each backup.

## 7. Be careful what you download

Malware usually arrives because someone ran it willingly.

- Install apps only from **official stores** and the developer's own website.
- Avoid cracked software, key generators and "free" versions of paid tools. They are one of the most common ways malware spreads.
- Be suspicious of files you did not ask for, even from friends, since their account may be compromised.
- If you are unsure about a file, scan it with a service like VirusTotal before opening it. It is not a guarantee, but it is a quick sanity check.

## 8. Think before you share

Attackers research their targets before they strike. Your birthday, your workplace, your pet's name, your holiday plans and your photos all help them craft a convincing message or guess a security question.

- Review the **privacy settings** on your social media accounts.
- Avoid posting travel plans in real time, and do not share photos of tickets, ID cards or boarding passes.
- Treat security questions as extra passwords. Answer them with random text and store the answers in your password manager.

## 9. Slow down when something feels urgent

Scammers rely on pressure. They pretend to be a boss, a bank, a delivery company or a family member in trouble. Today, voices and even video can be faked with AI tools, so a familiar voice is no longer proof of identity.

- **Pause.** Urgency is the biggest warning sign.
- **Verify through a second channel.** Hang up and call back on a number you already trust.
- Agree on a **family code word** for emergencies, so a cloned voice cannot fool your relatives.
- Never send money, gift cards or crypto because of an unexpected request.

## 10. Know what to do if you are compromised

Even careful people get caught sometimes. Acting quickly limits the damage.

1. **Disconnect** the affected device from the internet.
2. From a different, clean device, **change your passwords**, starting with your email, and turn on MFA.
3. **Contact your bank** if money or card details may be involved.
4. Check your email for unfamiliar **forwarding rules** and your accounts for logins you do not recognise, and sign out of all other sessions.
5. **Scan the device** with a trusted security tool, or restore it from a clean backup if needed.
6. **Report it.** Tell the platform, your workplace or school, and your national cyber emergency team (in Sri Lanka, that is Sri Lanka CERT).

---

## Your quick checklist

- A unique password for every account, kept in a password manager
- MFA turned on for email, banking and social media
- Automatic updates switched on
- Backups, with one copy kept offline
- A habit of **pausing before you click** and verifying through another channel
- Social media privacy settings reviewed

You do not have to do everything today. Start with your email account: a strong unique password and MFA. That single step closes the door attackers use most often.
